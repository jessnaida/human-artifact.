Is Technology Designed for Everyone?

Children and older adults can experience technology very differently. As digital platforms become more powerful, who is responsible for protecting the people who may be most vulnerable to their risks?

Not Every User Has the Same Relationship With Technology

Technology is advancing rapidly, but the people using it do not necessarily have the same experience, digital literacy, or ability to recognize its risks. Children and older adults can be vulnerable for different reasons.

Children & Teens

Can We Tell What's Real?

35%

of U.S. teens ages 13–18 said they had been misled by fake content online.

Another 22% said they had shared something they later learned was fake.

Children are growing up in an environment containing misinformation, manipulated media, and AI-generated content. Digital literacy is becoming increasingly important for deciding what should and should not be trusted.

Almost Constantly Online

46%

of U.S. teens said they were online almost constantly in 2024.

In 2015, that number was 24%.

That's an increase of 22 percentage points in less than a decade. Technology is not simply something many young people occasionally use; it can be a continuous part of their environment.

Older Adults

Fraud Has Real Consequences

$2.4B

Adults age 60 and older reported approximately $2.4 billion in fraud losses in 2024, according to the Federal Trade Commission.

New technologies can create opportunities for connection and independence, but they also create new forms of fraud and deception. Clearer warnings and easier-to-understand interfaces may help users make more informed decisions.

Digital Literacy Can Help

64% → 85%

In one study, older adults improved their ability to correctly distinguish true from false news after a one-hour digital media literacy intervention.

Vulnerability does not mean helplessness. Education and thoughtful technology design can give people more control over their digital experiences.

Who Should Adapt?

Should vulnerable users simply be expected to adapt to rapidly changing technology, or should technology be designed to adapt to the humans using it?

Platform Responsibility

Public Support for More Protection

85%

of U.S. adults surveyed by Pew Research Center in 2026 supported requiring parental consent for minors to create social media accounts.

78% supported age verification, and 78% supported limits on how much time minors can spend on social media.

Platforms often benefit from keeping users engaged. That creates an important question: when safety and engagement conflict, should companies decide where the line is, or should some protections be legally required?

What Could Better Design Look Like?

For Children

Age-appropriate recommendations, stronger privacy protections, parental controls, and reasonable screen-time safeguards.

For Older Adults

Clearer interfaces, understandable privacy settings, scam warnings, and explanations before information is made public.

For Everyone

Technology that prioritizes transparency, digital literacy, human agency, and informed choices.

A Question Worth Asking

If technology is truly designed for humans, shouldn't the humans who are most vulnerable to it be part of the design conversation?
